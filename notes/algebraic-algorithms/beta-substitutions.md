**Intent: formal semantic overview of substitution, β-reduction, and categorical equivalence.**

The central idea is:

> **Free-variable substitution is the syntactic operation that implements reindexing of a term along an assignment of variables. β-reduction is the computational law saying that applying a λ-abstraction to an argument is equivalent to substituting that argument for the abstraction's bound variable. In category theory, this becomes evaluation plus the universal property of exponentials, and substitution is represented by pullback/reindexing.**

There are several levels that should be kept distinct.

---

# 1. Syntax: variables, terms, and free variables

Take the simply typed λ-calculus:

$$
\Gamma \vdash t : A
$$

where:

* $\Gamma$ is a context of typed variables,
* $t$ is a term,
* $A$ is its type.

For example:

$$
\Gamma = x:A,\ y:B
$$

and

$$
\Gamma \vdash f(x,y):C.
$$

The **free variables** of a term are defined recursively:

$$
FV(x)=\{x\}
$$

$$
FV(c)=\varnothing
$$

$$
FV(tu)=FV(t)\cup FV(u)
$$

$$
FV(\lambda x.t)=FV(t)\setminus\{x\}.
$$

Thus:

$$
FV(\lambda x.f(x,y))=\{f,y\}
$$

assuming $f$ is itself a variable.

The variable $x$ occurs syntactically inside the body but is **bound**, not free.

---

# 2. Free-variable substitution

The notation

$$
t[x:=u]
$$

means:

> Replace every **free occurrence** of $x$ in $t$ by $u$.

The qualification "free" is absolutely essential.

For example:

$$
(x+y)[x:=z] = z+y
$$

but:

$$
(\lambda x.(x+y))[x:=z]
=\lambda x.(x+y).
$$

The $x$ inside the abstraction is bound by $\lambda x$ and therefore is not substituted.

The recursive definition is:

$$
x[x:=u]=u
$$

$$
y[x:=u]=y
\qquad
(y\neq x)
$$

$$
(t_1t_2)[x:=u]
=t_1[x:=u]\;t_2[x:=u]
$$

and:

$$
(\lambda y.t)[x:=u]
=\lambda y.(t[x:=u])
$$

provided that $y$ does not occur freely in $u$.

If $y\in FV(u)$, naïve substitution would cause **variable capture**.

For example:

$$
(\lambda y.x)[x:=y]
$$

cannot simply become:

$$
\lambda y.y
$$

because the substituted $y$ would become bound by the surrounding $\lambda y$.

The correct operation is **α-renaming** first:

$$
\lambda y.x
\equiv_\alpha
\lambda z.x
$$

then:

$$
(\lambda z.x)[x:=y]
=\lambda z.y.
$$

So the real substitution operation is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{substitute freely while avoiding capture}
}
$$

---

# 3. The substitution lemma

The fundamental theorem is the **substitution lemma**.

If:

$$
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B
$$

and:

$$
\Delta\vdash u:A
$$

then:

$$
\Gamma,\Delta\vdash t[x:=u]:B
$$

under the appropriate context compatibility conditions.

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A term with a variable}
+
\text{a term supplying that variable}
\longrightarrow
\text{a new term with the variable eliminated}
}
$$

The context

$$
\Gamma,x:A
$$

is a term with a "hole" of type $A$.

The term $u:A$ fills that hole.

This is already very close to category theory.

---

# 4. β-substitution

The β-rule is:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u
\longrightarrow_\beta
t[x:=u].
$$

For example:

$$
(\lambda x.x+1)5
\longrightarrow_\beta
5+1.
$$

Or:

$$
(\lambda x.\lambda y.x)a
\longrightarrow_\beta
\lambda y.a.
$$

The second argument then gives:

$$
(\lambda y.a)b
\longrightarrow_\beta
a.
$$

The important point is:

$$
\boxed{
\beta\text{-reduction is not an arbitrary rewrite}
}
$$

It is the computational interpretation of **function application**.

The abstraction:

$$
\lambda x.t
$$

constructs a function.

The application:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u
$$

feeds the input $u$ into that function.

β-reduction says:

$$
\boxed{
\text{apply the function}
:\ \text{replace its input variable by the argument}
}
$$

---

# 5. β-equivalence

The one-step relation is:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u
\to_\beta
t[x:=u].
$$

The corresponding equivalence relation is generated by:

$$
\equiv_\beta
$$

where we allow β-reduction in either direction and inside arbitrary contexts.

Thus:

$$
(\lambda x.x)a
\equiv_\beta
a
$$

and:

$$
a
\equiv_\beta
(\lambda x.x)a.
$$

The difference is:

$$
\to_\beta
\quad\text{is directed computation}
$$

whereas:

$$
\equiv_\beta
\quad\text{is symmetric equality generated by computation}.
$$

Usually one also includes α-equivalence:

$$
\equiv_{\alpha\beta}.
$$

Thus:

$$
\lambda x.x
\equiv_\alpha
\lambda y.y
$$

and:

$$
(\lambda x.x)a
\equiv_{\alpha\beta}
a.
$$

---

# 6. The key distinction: syntax versus semantics

There are three different notions that are often conflated.

## Syntactic substitution

$$
t[x:=u]
$$

This is a recursively defined operation on syntax.

## β-reduction

$$
(\lambda x.t)u
\to_\beta
t[x:=u]
$$

This is a computational rewrite.

## Semantic equality

Two terms may denote the same morphism:

$$
\llbracket t\rrbracket
=\llbracket u\rrbracket.
$$

The fundamental soundness property is:

$$
t\equiv_{\alpha\beta}u
\implies
\llbracket t\rrbracket
=\llbracket u\rrbracket.
$$

So β-equivalence is a **syntactic approximation to semantic equality**.

---

# 7. Categorical semantics: contexts are objects

In categorical semantics, a context is interpreted as an object:

$$
\llbracket \Gamma\rrbracket = X.
$$

A typed term:

$$
\Gamma\vdash t:A
$$

is interpreted as a morphism:

$$
\llbracket t\rrbracket:X\to A.
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{context}
\longmapsto
\text{object}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{term in context}
\longmapsto
\text{morphism}
}
$$

For example:

$$
x:A,y:B\vdash t:C
$$

becomes:

$$
\llbracket A\rrbracket\times\llbracket B\rrbracket
\longrightarrow
\llbracket C\rrbracket.
$$

The variables are projections:

$$
\pi_1:A\times B\to A
$$

and:

$$
\pi_2:A\times B\to B.
$$

So the syntactic variable $x$ is categorically the projection:

$$
x
\quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
\pi_1.
$$

This is the first major categorical reinterpretation.

---

# 8. Substitution is composition

Suppose:

$$
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B.
$$

Categorically:

$$
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\times A
\overset{\llbracket t\rrbracket}{\longrightarrow}
B.
$$

Now suppose:

$$
\Gamma\vdash u:A.
$$

Categorically:

$$
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket
\overset{\llbracket u\rrbracket}{\longrightarrow}
A.
$$

To substitute $u$ for $x$, we construct:

$$
\langle id_\Gamma,\llbracket u\rrbracket\rangle
:
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket
\to
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\times A.
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{
\llbracket t[x:=u]\rrbracket
=\llbracket t\rrbracket
\circ
\langle id_\Gamma,\llbracket u\rrbracket\rangle
}
$$

This is the categorical form of substitution.

In words:

> First construct the context together with the supplied value $u$, then evaluate the original term.

The syntactic operation:

$$
t[x:=u]
$$

becomes categorical composition.

This is the deepest basic correspondence:

$$
\boxed{
\text{substitution}
;\longleftrightarrow;
\text{reindexing / composition}
}
$$

---

# 9. λ-abstraction: currying

Suppose:

$$
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B.
$$

Categorically:

$$
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\times A
\to B.
$$

If the category is **cartesian closed**, there is an exponential object:

$$
B^A.
$$

The universal property gives a bijection:

$$
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma\times A,B)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,B^A).
$$

Thus the λ-abstraction:

$$
\lambda x.t
$$

is interpreted as the **curried morphism**:

$$
\Lambda(\llbracket t\rrbracket):
\Gamma\to B^A.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\lambda x.t
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{curry}(t)
}
$$

This is not merely an analogy. In the categorical semantics of the simply typed λ-calculus, this is literally the interpretation.

---

# 10. Application: evaluation

The exponential object comes with an evaluation morphism:

$$
\operatorname{ev}_{A,B}
:
B^A\times A
\to B.
$$

The application:

$$
f,u
$$

is interpreted as:

$$
\operatorname{ev}
\circ
\langle f,u\rangle.
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{application}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{evaluation morphism}
}
$$

Given:

$$
f:\Gamma\to B^A
$$

and:

$$
u:\Gamma\to A,
$$

we form:

$$
\langle f,u\rangle:
\Gamma\to B^A\times A
$$

then:

$$
\Gamma
\overset{\langle f,u\rangle}{\longrightarrow}
B^A\times A
\overset{\operatorname{ev}}{\longrightarrow}
B.
$$

---

# 11. β-equivalence is the exponential universal property

Now we can state the central categorical fact.

Suppose:

$$
t:\Gamma\times A\to B.
$$

Its currying is:

$$
\Lambda(t):\Gamma\to B^A.
$$

The β-law says:

$$
\operatorname{ev}
\circ
\langle\Lambda(t),id_A\rangle
=t.
$$

More generally, if:

$$
u:\Gamma\to A,
$$

then:

$$
\operatorname{ev}
\circ
\langle\Lambda(t),u\rangle
=t
\circ
\langle id_\Gamma,u\rangle.
$$

The left side is categorical function application:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u.
$$

The right side is categorical substitution:

$$
t[x:=u].
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{ev}
\circ
\langle\Lambda(t),u\rangle
=t
\circ
\langle id,u\rangle
}
$$

is precisely the categorical β-law.

The correspondence is:

| λ-calculus     | Cartesian closed category |
| -------------- | ------------------------- |
| $x$            | projection                |
| $t[x:=u]$      | composition/reindexing    |
| $\lambda x.t$  | currying                  |
| $fu$           | evaluation                |
| β-reduction    | exponential β-law         |
| η-equivalence  | exponential η-law         |
| βη-equivalence | equality of morphisms     |

---

# 12. The β-law and η-law

The β-law:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u
\equiv_\beta
t[x:=u].
$$

Categorically:

$$
\operatorname{ev}
\circ
\langle\Lambda(t),u\rangle
=t
\circ
\langle id,u\rangle.
$$

The η-law is:

$$
\lambda x.f,x
\equiv_\eta
f
\qquad
x\notin FV(f).
$$

Categorically:

$$
\Lambda(\operatorname{ev}\circ\langle f\circ\pi_1,\pi_2\rangle)
=f.
$$

The β and η laws together express that currying and evaluation are mutually inverse:

$$
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma\times A,B)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,B^A).
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\beta\eta\text{-equivalence}
\approx
\text{the universal property of exponentials}
}
$$

# 13. Substitution as reindexing: the more general categorical picture

The previous treatment uses a cartesian closed category. The more general perspective is **categorical logic**.

A context $\Gamma$ has a category of terms/types over it:

$$
\mathcal{C}/\Gamma.
$$

A substitution:

$$
\sigma:\Delta\to\Gamma
$$

induces a reindexing operation:

$$
\sigma^\ast:
\mathcal{C}/\Gamma
\to
\mathcal{C}/\Delta.
$$

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{
\text{change of variables}
:\ \text{pullback along a morphism}
}
$$

For a family:

$$
A\to\Gamma,
$$

substitution along:

$$
\sigma:\Delta\to\Gamma
$$

produces:

$$
\sigma^\ast A
=\Delta\times_\Gamma A.
$$

This is the categorical version of replacing variables according to $\sigma$.

Thus the general structure is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{contexts}
\quad+\quad
\text{substitutions}
\quad+\quad
\text{terms}
}
$$

represented by a **category with families**, a **contextual category**, or related categorical structure.

# 14. The crucial distinction: substitution is not β-reduction

This is worth making extremely explicit.

Suppose:

$$
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B
$$

and:

$$
\Gamma\vdash u:A.
$$

Then:

$$
t[x:=u]
$$

is valid whether or not $t$ contains a λ-abstraction.

For example:

$$
(x+1)[x:=5]=5+1.
$$

This is just substitution.

β-reduction requires a particular syntactic shape:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u.
$$

Then:

$$
(\lambda x.t)u
\to_\beta
t[x:=u].
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\beta\text{-reduction uses substitution}
}
$$

but:

$$
\boxed{
\text{substitution is more general than β-reduction}
}
$$

Categorically:

* substitution is reindexing/composition;
* β-reduction is the compatibility between currying and evaluation.

# 16. The deepest perspective: substitution is the primitive operation

A very useful way to think about it is that **variables are not fundamentally values**.

A variable is a **projection from a context**.

For:

$$
\Gamma=(x_1:A_1,\ldots,x_n:A_n),
$$

the variable $x_i$ is:

$$
\pi_i:
A_1\times\cdots\times A_n
\to A_i.
$$

A term is a morphism built from these projections.

When you substitute:

$$
x_i:=u_i,
$$

you construct the morphism:

$$
\langle u_1,\ldots,u_n\rangle
:
\Delta
\to
\Gamma.
$$

Then every term:

$$
t:\Gamma\to B
$$

is reindexed:

$$
\Delta
\overset{\langle u_1,\ldots,u_n\rangle}{\longrightarrow}
\Gamma
\overset{t}{\longrightarrow}
B.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
t[u_1/x_1,\ldots,u_n/x_n]
=t\circ \langle u_1,\ldots,u_n\rangle
}
$$

This is the categorical master equation for simultaneous substitution.

And β-reduction is the special case where the term $t$ has first been packaged into an exponential object via currying, then immediately evaluated.

So, at the highest level:

> **Substitution is the fundamental semantic operation. λ-abstraction packages a substitution-dependent term into a morphism-valued object. Application unpacks it. β-equivalence says that packaging and immediately unpacking preserves the original substitution.**
