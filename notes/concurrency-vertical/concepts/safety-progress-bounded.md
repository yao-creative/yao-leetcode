You're very close to the actual proof strategy used in concurrent algorithms. The key idea is that **Peterson's algorithm is not proved by simply saying "LockOne ∧ LockTwo."** Instead, it **reuses predicates and invariants** from the simpler algorithms, but the proof must be carried out over the **combined state machine**.

Let's formalize the properties first.

---

# System model

Let

* Threads

$$
T = {0,1}
$$

Each thread has a control state

$$
pc_i \in
{\text{Idle},\text{Trying},\text{Waiting},\text{CS},\text{Exit}}
$$

Shared state

$$
S=(flag_0,flag_1,victim)
$$

The global state is

$$
G=(pc_0,pc_1,S).
$$

A transition is

$$
G
\rightarrow
G'.
$$

---

# Safety

Safety means

> Nothing bad ever happens.

The bad state is

$$
Bad
===

(pc_0=CS)
\land
(pc_1=CS)
$$

Safety is

$$
\forall \pi,\forall t,
\neg Bad(\pi(t))
$$

where

* (\pi) is an execution,
* (t) is a point in time.

Equivalently,

$$
|{i\mid pc_i=CS}|\le1.
$$

This is mutual exclusion.

---

# Progress

Progress is a liveness property.

Suppose

* nobody is inside the critical section

$$
\forall i,;
pc_i\neq CS
$$

and

someone wants to enter

$$
\exists i,;
pc_i=Trying.
$$

Then

eventually

$$
\Diamond
(pc_i=CS)
$$

for some requesting thread.

Using temporal logic,

$$
(\exists i,Trying_i)
\land
(\forall j,\neg CS_j)
\Rightarrow
\Diamond
(\exists k,CS_k).
$$

No deadlock.

---

# Bounded waiting

Suppose thread (i) requests entry.

After that,

other threads can enter only finitely many times.

One formulation is

there exists

$$
N
$$

such that

$$
#(\text{other entries})
\le N.
$$

For Peterson,

$$
N=1.
$$

The other thread may enter once,

but cannot repeatedly overtake you forever.

---

# Why Peterson is not literally

$$
LockOne
\land
LockTwo
$$

This is the subtle point.

The **algorithm** is

```python
flag[i]=True
victim=i

while flag[other] and victim==i:
    pass
```

Notice the waiting predicate

$$
P_i
===

flag_{other}
\land
(victim=i).
$$

This predicate is indeed

the conjunction of

LockOne's waiting condition

$$
L_1
===

flag_{other}
$$

and

LockTwo's

$$
L_2
===

(victim=i).
$$

So the **guard** is

$$
P_i=L_1\land L_2.
$$

This is true.

---

# But the proof is not

The proof is **not**

$$
Proof(LockOne)
\land
Proof(LockTwo).
$$

Why?

Because

LockOne is deadlocking.

LockTwo violates progress.

You cannot combine invalid proofs into a valid proof.

Instead,

you define a **new invariant** over the product state

$$
(flag_0,flag_1,victim).
$$

---

# Safety proof

Assume both threads are inside.

Then both loops terminated.

For thread 0,

termination means

$$
\neg
(flag_1
\land
victim=0).
$$

Therefore

$$
\neg flag_1
\lor
victim\neq0.
$$

Similarly

$$
\neg flag_0
\lor
victim\neq1.
$$

Inside the critical section,

both flags remain True,

so

$$
flag_0
======

# flag_1

True.
$$

Thus

$$
victim\neq0
$$

and

$$
victim\neq1.
$$

Impossible,

because

$$
victim
\in
{0,1}.
$$

Contradiction.

Therefore

both cannot be inside.

---

# Reusing previous predicates

This is where your intuition is correct.

You can think of Peterson as **lifting** two predicates.

Intent predicate

$$
I_i
===

flag_i.
$$

Arbitration predicate

$$
A_i
===

(victim=i).
$$

Waiting predicate

$$
W_i
===

I_{other}
\land
A_i.
$$

These predicates come directly from

* LockOne

and

* LockTwo.

The correctness proof then studies

the transition system generated by

$$
(I,A)
$$

rather than proving the original algorithms independently.

---

# Category-theoretic view

This is actually a beautiful example of composition.

Each algorithm is a state machine

$$
M_1=(S_1,\delta_1)
$$

and

$$
M_2=(S_2,\delta_2).
$$

Peterson is essentially their synchronized product

$$
M
=

M_1
\times
M_2.
$$

The state is

$$
S=S_1\times S_2.
$$

The guard is

$$
Guard
=====

Guard_1
\land
Guard_2.
$$

The proof obligation is **not** inherited automatically. Instead, you prove that the **product machine** preserves a stronger global invariant. This is a common pattern in concurrent and distributed systems: compose protocols by taking the product of their state machines, then prove properties of the composed system, often reusing local predicates from the components but establishing new global invariants over the combined state. This is why your observation about the `and` operator is insightful—it identifies how the *guards* compose, even though the *correctness proof* must be carried out on the composed protocol itself.
